            <legend>Programming and Taste</legend>
            <p>People who program like writing a lot about things they aren't trained in or don't understand. Unfortunately I am not exempt from that desire. But first, movie reviews.</p>
            <h2>Frankenstein (2025) and Kes (1969)</h2>
            <p>Bear with me. I watched roughly the first half of del Toro's Frankenstein before I stopped. I don't think it's my movie. The man is a beautiful artist and has an eye for monsters. Frankenstein himself (itself?) is an ethereal creation. It moves and emotes in a stilted way, writhing in its surroundings and shackles as the early born hunk of flesh he was. But I could not stand Victor. This is clearly by design. It's Frankenstein, Victor is consumed by his own desires and screams all of these in dramatic proclamations us forced to listen. We can watch him in all his physical and mental anguish when he fails and feel something I guess. The display is graphic and imports effort beyond anything we ever need to see. I think it wasn't for me.</p>
            <p>I quit out and watched Kes. It's a more reserved. It follows a young boy Casper, who by all accounts is a brat. Everyone in the film seems deeply flawed in some way. It takes place in mining town in the North (of England), people are on edge. There isn't some grand plot, watch watch Casper go through his life and feel all his hardships and the small joys little bits of compassion give. I liked the film. Some terrible preference towards slice of life.</p>
            <p>They are different films for different people. I should try the first film again sometime, maybe after watching more of del Toro's work like Pan's Labyrinth. It'll acclimate myself to his style and I can enjoy Frankenstein. Or maybe not, idk.</p>
            <p>The point is I like things and the reasons are weird an fuzzy. I can theory craft why I hated Victor's dramatic proclamations, it would probably be pretty fun, but get nowhere doing much useful. Let's try anyway. The center piece of del Toro's films are his intricate visuals. They capture detail and nuance in the characters and their setting. Victor is this smart guy slob and William is passive and "proper" to the point of smothering himself. Then del Toro has Victor scream into the air atop his tower or splotches red again and again over characters' outfits to tell us things we already know. After drawing his painting, del Toro wrote in big permanent marker on top of it. It's a contrast of camp with legitimate attempts at story telling which creates irreconcilable contrast. Well, for me. So many words and we've ended up back where we started: the movie wasn't right for me right now.</p>
            <h2>Programming?</h2>
            <p>Want to talk about programming? Yeah, me to. The problem is when doing so, so many people want to avoid those last couple of words I appended to my analysis of Frankenstein, "for me," Arguments about features and footguns (sometimes in Greek) about whose right. The zealots missed some lesson on media subjectivity, because everything can be viewed as media and everything is subjective.</p>
            <p>Is that a controversial statement?</p>
            <p>Here, take the age old debate considering functional vs. iterative traversals, say collecting into an accumulator. A fold doesn't do much different than a simple loop. The accumulation function is the same as a loop body, it's arguments corresponding to mutable variables in the iterative approach and the body corresponding to the loop body. If the fold gets optimized to be tail recursive, the function arguments basically look mutable like the loop (of course a bit depending on implementation). These two structures have the same data, simply presented in different places. Some prefer using a fold and some prefer using a loop. Maybe the same person will prefer one in one context and the other in a different context. Actually, I hope that would be true. It's simply a matter of taste and preference what feels more readable.</p>
            <p>Even in cases where there isn't an equal presentation of information, preference can come down to a matter of taste. Consider languages with strong, expressive type systems (e.g. Rust, Haskell, OCaml) and languages with minimal type systems (e.g. C). It is reasonable to say code without a bunch of annotations asking the compiler to check things is simpler than languages with complicated type annotations everywhere. At the same time, I can say I think your taste is wrong and bad and you need to change.</p>
            <h2>You Have Bad Taste</h2>
            <p>Is it a bad thing to assert people can have bad taste? Saying that well is hard, especially to a person with bad taste. Bad taste of course is different than liking "trash". I read trashy sci-fi and fanfics and they're great. It isn't me, but people watch all types of shitty B movies and that's great. I love a stupid bash script and pearl has a great many fans. There are ways these unpolished works expose their creators for what they are or play and exemplify parts of their medium or world. Maybe they can be plain fun. But I want to contrast these with, for example, an almost idolization of some of the physicists on the Manhattan Project (read Richard Feynman). Maybe there is some amount of American pride in the project, but at the end of the day it was a big weapons manufacturing project with some pretty big assholes on it. A positive fascination in it betrays some sort of ignorance to that or at least to me some amount of disagreeable politics. By example, this is what I mean by bad taste. Not a taste for bad things, but a taste perpetuating or at least espousing harm. Of course this is still subjective, isn't everything being subjective one of the theses of this piece, but I'd like to hope we can agree some broad strokes here for what is bad and what is good. Maybe stepping all the way back, we can probably agree a taste amenable to cats is good and one amenable to nazis is bad. Well it's 2026 so maybe we can't hope even that anymore, but you know what I mean.</p>
            <p>So a taste for code writing down less, asking machines and automations to do less, to me, often (but not always!) betrays some amount of ignorance of technologies or an arrogance in the face their fallible human form. The result is buggy software and hostile developer environments. Sounds like harm to me.</p>
            <p>Moral of the story here isn't that C is a terrible language and you should never write it and you should write Rust instead, but to think about your taste, what it means, and where it comes from. Peace.</p>
